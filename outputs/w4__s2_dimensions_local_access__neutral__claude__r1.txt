Short answer: **not confirmed either way yet** — and "oversize permit" isn't actually the right lens here. N.J.A.C. 16:32 isn't a weight/dimension permit scheme; it's a *route-designation* rule, and whether it even applies to your rig depends on a fact you haven't nailed down yet: **loaded width**.

**Why width is the threshold question**

N.J.A.C. 16:32 restricts the routing of only two categories of vehicles:
- Double-trailer truck combinations, and
- "102-inch-wide standard trucks" — defined as vehicles **wider than 96 inches, up to 102 inches**

A single-trailer tractor-semitrailer that happens to be 96 inches (8 ft) or narrower isn't covered by this chapter at all, and CR 624's absence from Appendix C would be irrelevant. But almost all modern dry vans and reefers are built to the full 102-inch (8'6") legal standard width — which is not "oversize," it's ordinary legal width — and if yours is, this chapter's route rules apply to your trip. You need the actual loaded width before this question can be answered.

**How the network works, and what "not in Appendix C" means**

Assuming the 102-inch threshold is met, the truck must run the **National Network** (Interstates, NJ Turnpike, AC Expressway) except where it diverts onto the **NJ Access Network** to reach a terminal by the direct route, or for reasonable access to food/fuel/rest/repairs within a short mileage of the designated system.

The Access Network is built from three pieces:
- All state highways, *except* those excluded in Appendix A
- All county "500‑series" roads, *except* those excluded in Appendix B
- County **"600‑series"** roads — but *only those listed in Appendix C*

Appendix C is an **inclusion list**, not an exclusion list like A and B. That structural difference matters: a 500-series road is presumed *in* unless excluded; a 600-series road is presumed *out* unless affirmatively listed. CR 624 is a 600-series county route. If it's genuinely absent from Appendix C, it is **not part of the designated Access Network** — it isn't a gray area, it's simply not on the list.

**Does that flatly prohibit the route?**

Not necessarily — there are two possible saves, neither of which you can confirm from your desk right now:

1. *Direct-route-to-terminal doctrine*: the rule lets a covered truck leave the network "by the direct route" to reach its terminal, and last-mile local municipal streets are generally understood as part of that direct route rather than as separate network segments. But CR 624 is a numbered county route, not an unclassified local street — that's exactly the category the regulation chose to control by listing, so a claim of "it's just part of my direct route" is much weaker for a listed-by-number county road than for an ordinary city street.
2. *Federal reasonable-access preemption* (23 CFR 658.19): states can't deny STAA-dimensioned vehicles reasonable access between the National Network and a terminal. If your rig or trailer configuration independently qualifies as an STAA-authorized vehicle, that federal floor could override a state listing gap — but this is a fact-specific determination, not something to assume at the dispatch desk.

- If >96" (very likely), don't route the last leg over CR 624 on the assumption it's fine.
